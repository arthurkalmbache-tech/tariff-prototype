Chapter 22
Chapter 22 is the customs chapter every bottle of wine on earth travels under: beverages, spirits and vinegar.
Read my catalogue

Send Chapter 22 your catalogue and it sends you back a briefing. It reads the document itself — the one you already send to buyers — then tests every published tariff, duty and trade measure in the markets you ship to against your own lines, scores what it finds out of a hundred, and writes up the rest: what each measure names, what it costs a bottle, and, just as plainly, what your catalogue cannot tell it. It does not forecast, and it does not fill a gap with an estimate.

What follows is a demonstration rather than a brochure, and it runs in this order: first the working — Acts 01 to 04, one question at a time, every figure arrived at in front of you rather than announced. Then, near the foot of the page, the finished briefing itself, written against the book of one illustrative exporter — eleven wines, his live orders in each market, and real measures as published. It is long and it is somebody else’s, so it sits folded until you ask for it. Then you hand over your own catalogue and it reads that instead. One honest note, since it matters: the document reading is real, and happens in your browser rather than on a server. The regulatory matching and the exposure score run against a fixed set of five markets rather than a live feed.

First question: does the wording name your bottles?

Trade measures are never written in grape varieties. They are written in customs codes and country of origin, which is why reading the news tells you a measure exists but not whether it reaches you. So the first thing Chapter 22 does is mechanical: it takes the measure exactly as published and tests it against your list, one bottle at a time. This is the step behind the reaches so many of your lines figure in every market block of the briefing at the foot of this page — here it is first, line by line, so you can see it rather than take it.

Second question: does this kind of thing actually arrive?

Knowing a measure names your bottles is not the same as knowing it will ever bite. Some announcements become law in weeks; some are traded away in the negotiation they were invented for. Chapter 22 will not tell you which this one is, because nobody honestly can. What it will do is show you every comparable measure in this market and how far each one actually got, so you can judge for yourself. The briefing gives each measure its status and its date and stops there, on purpose. This is where that stopping point is explained.

Third question: at what point does the business stop working?

"A tariff" is four different things depending on where you ship, and each bites in a different place: a percentage of your invoice, a charge on the alcohol in the bottle, a duty you can escape by raising your price, or a shelf you are simply removed from. Chapter 22 models the one that actually applies in the market you have chosen, against this exporter's own open orders, and finds the level at which the money runs out. Note the input: open orders. A catalogue does not contain them, which is precisely why the briefing reports this figure as something it cannot determine from your document. This is the working it would need.

04

Then you decide — and here is what Chapter 22 will never do for you

You now have the three facts: which of your lines the wording names, how far measures of this kind have actually got in this market, and the rate at which the arithmetic stops working. The decision after that is yours, and it should be. A tool that pretended otherwise would be worse than no tool, so here is the boundary — stated plainly, and stated here rather than after you have handed anything over.

It won't predictNo likelihood scores, no confidence percentages. Whether a measure lands is a political judgement, and dressing a guess in a number would only make you trust it more than you should.
It won't classify or adviseYour codes come from you or your forwarder. Chapter 22 reads measures against them; it is not a customs broker and it does not give legal advice.
It won't speak to your buyersThe conversation with your importer is the whole relationship. You have that conversation. Chapter 22 makes sure you walk into it already knowing the arithmetic.
It won't touch your accountsCosts and prices are typed in once, by you, and stay in your own workspace. No ledger connection, no bank access, nothing to breach.

I built this after an exporter described his March 2025 to me: weeks spent reading coverage of a 200% tariff that was never published, while the 15% that did arrive reached him through his importer. He was not short of a forecast. He was short of three answers — which of his own lines the wording named, whether measures of that kind had ever actually arrived in that market, and the rate at which his price stops working. Those three things are all this page does. If they are not what costs you your weeks, tell me that and I will stop building it.

Before the figures · whose book this is

You should meet the exporter before you read his briefing.

Maison Rivaud is a négociant in Burgundy, and it does not exist. It was invented for this page, so that every figure below can be checked against a catalogue printed in full rather than taken on trust, and so that no real exporter’s trading position ends up on a public website. Everything else about it is ordinary. It bottles five wines from its own stretch of the Côte and the Chablisien — a Gevrey-Chambertin premier cru, a Chablis premier cru, a Mercurey, a Crémant and a marc distilled from its own pomace — and buys in six more from growers it has dealt with for years: Rioja, Rías Baixas, Barolo, Chianti, the Douro and the Vinho Verde.

62,100 bottles a year is small. It means one person doing the customs paperwork, an owner who knows every grower by name, and a margin a single tariff can take apart. It is also the exact size at which this problem is worst: too small to keep a customs adviser on a retainer, too large for a bad quarter to be absorbed in silence.

He ships to five countries, and five orders are open on the book today — two American, to importers in Chicago and Boston; one Chinese, to a spirits house in Shanghai; two Indian, in Mumbai and Bengaluru. Nothing at all in Britain or Canada. That absence gets used rather than ignored: no open order means no invoice to model, so for two of his five markets the briefing says exactly that and scores what it can from the catalogue alone.

One last thing, and it is the point. His catalogue has two holes in it. The Chianti was printed without a customs code, and the Vinho Verde without a strength — the sort of omission in almost every real export list, because the list was written for buyers rather than for customs. Both are left exactly as the document prints them. What the briefing does with those two lines, and what it flatly refuses to do, is the part of it worth your attention.

Step two · the account your watch belongs to

The briefing is not posted anywhere. It is written into your account. That is deliberate: a tariff notice is worth something for about a week, and an email sitting unread in a folder is worth nothing at all. So the reading lands in one place you can open, and when a measure moves afterwards the same place tells you what moved — against the figures you have already seen, not against a fresh guess.

Ten characters or more, and not a word on its own

Read a document above and this unlocks. The first reading and the first month of watching cost nothing.

Been here before?

  1. You hand it over once. A PDF or a spreadsheet, whichever you already have. Line, customs code, origin, strength, your cost. Nothing else.
  2. It watches the official sources, not the news. Published notices and official journals in every market you ship to, re-read against your own lines rather than against a keyword.
  3. When something moves, it appears in your watch as a figure, not a link. Which of your lines the new wording names, what it does to the number above, and what it does not tell us — said as plainly as what it does.

Silence the rest of the time. If a month goes by with nothing published that touches your bottles, your watch stays empty — and that is the service working, not the service broken.
